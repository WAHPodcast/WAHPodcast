Episode 1: The Women's War

In this inaugural episode of WAH-Pod, historian Nwando Achebe—joined by co-hosts Henry Fagan and Emmanuel Ankomahene—recounts the Women’s War of 1929, one of the largest women-led anti-colonial uprisings in African history. Dismissively named the “Aba Women’s Riot” by British colonial authorities, the Women’s War was a coordinated resistance movement involving tens of thousands of Igbo and Ibibio women in southeastern Nigeria. Drawing on oral histories, colonial archives, and indigenous political systems, the episode challenges colonial portrayals of the uprising as spontaneous disorder, revealing instead a deliberate and organized defense of women’s economic interests, political authority, and established systems of accountability.

Episode 2: Yaa Asantewaa and The Golden Stool

In the second episode of WAH-Pod, Achebe, Fagan, and Ankomahene tell the story of Yaa Asantewaa, the formidable queen mother who led Asante resistance against British colonial rule in 1900. At the heart of this struggle was the Golden Stool, the sacred symbol of the Asante nation's unity, sovereignty, and spiritual identity. When British Governor Frederick Hodgson demanded possession of the Golden Stool, he provoked a powerful resistance led by Yaa Asantewaa. Drawing on Asante history and traditions, the episode explores the political and spiritual significance of the Golden Stool, the extraordinary leadership of Yaa Asantewaa, and the struggle to defend Asante sovereignty. It is a story that reminds us that the symbols through which a people understand their history, culture, and identity can hold meaning far beyond their material form—and that imperial power has limits.

Episode 3: Britain's Punitive Expedition: How Benin was Stripped of Its Wealth

This episode tells the story of Britain’s invasion of the Kingdom of Benin (in present-day southern Nigeria) in 1897. We explore how Benin’s autonomy, political order, and wealth made it a target of British imperialism. When Acting Consul James Phillips disregarded warnings not to enter Benin during the sacred festival of Igue, a violent confrontation followed, providing Britain with a pretext for invasion. Following Benin’s defeat, British forces looted the city, seizing thousands of royal and sacred treasures, many of which were subsequently sold to help finance the expedition. The episode explores how the material expressions of Benin’s sacred kingship were transformed into commodities and how British imperial intrusion into African political and economic sovereignty provoked resistance that was then used to justify conquest. Ultimately, it asks what happens when an empire seizes not only a people’s independence but also the treasures through which a civilization remembers and governs itself.

Episode 4: The Day the Soldiers Came for Fela

This episode tells the story of how Afrobeat, a musical form pioneered by Fela Anikulapo Kuti, became a powerful vehicle of resistance that challenged Nigeria’s military dictatorship. Through his music, performances, and outspoken criticism of government corruption and military brutality, Fela emerged as one of Nigeria’s most provocative and influential political voices. The episode explores what happened in February 1977, when Nigerian soldiers stormed Kalakuta Republic, Fela’s communal compound in Lagos, unleashing violence against its residents and destroying the compound. The attack became one of the defining confrontations between Fela and the Nigerian military government. Through this extraordinary story, we explore the power of music as political resistance and the lengths to which authoritarian governments will go to silence dissent.
