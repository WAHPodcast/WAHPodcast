Going Deeper

The encounter at Oloko: In Oloko, in present-day Abia State, Emeruwa sought to count the members of Nwayeruwa’s household and her property. Nwayeruwa, a widow, challenged him. For women who feared that counting would lead to taxation, the encounter raised a question that reached far beyond her household. As word spread, women traveled to Oloko to press their demands.

Warrant Chiefs and indirect Rule: Under a system the British called “indirect rule,” colonial officials appointed local leaders to govern on their behalf. Often young and ambitious, these men held little traditional authority in their communities. The British warrant gave them power their communities had never granted them. Many used that power for personal gain, demanding bribes, seizing land, and imposing forced labor. Women were often particular targets of these abuses. For many women, warrant chiefs were the local face of a corrupt colonial system that threatened their livelihoods, eroded their standing in the community, and undermined the traditions through which they exercised authority.

"Sitting on a Man": To “sit on a man” was the strongest sanction Igbo women had for punishing wrongdoing. Also practiced among Ibibio women, it played a central role in the Women’s War of 1929. Women would gather at the offending man’s compound and surround his house, singing, chanting, and dancing as they named his offense and held him up to public ridicule. Sometimes they plastered the walls with mud. Sometimes they tore the house apart, thatching and all. They would draw the man out of his compound and encircle him, keeping up their songs and taunts until he acknowledged what he had done and agreed to make amends. The women could also expose their nakedness, an act whose gravity lay in Igbo understandings of motherhood and spiritual power. For a mother to bare herself publicly before the man who had wronged her was to invoke a spiritual sanction. During the Women’s War, women drew on this familiar means of holding an offender to account and turned it against warrant chiefs who exercised power in the name of the British.

Anthropology and Power: The Women’s War forced British officials to reckon with how little they knew about the people they claimed to govern. They knew which men held warrants because they had issued those warrants themselves. What they had failed to grasp was how women exercised authority. Who could call women together? How did word of a grievance travel from one community to the next? How could an encounter in Oloko bring women across southeastern Nigeria into action? After 1929, these questions took on new urgency for a colonial administration that the women had caught by surprise. The British commissioned the government anthropologist C. K. Meek to study Indigenous political and judicial institutions. Sylvia Leith-Ross and Margaret Green also undertook fieldwork on Igbo society, while the linguist Ida C. Ward pursued a separate study of language. Colonial administrative officers, meanwhile, compiled some 200 intelligence reports by 1934. The word intelligence is worth pausing over. For the women, the ability to call a meeting, carry word to neighboring communities, and act together was part of political life. For British officials, those same activities had become matters for investigation and monitoring. Officials drew on what they learned to reorganize colonial courts and local government. They also hoped to recognize the beginnings of another mobilization before women could again confront them on such a scale.


In the Classroom

Engaging the Episode

  • • Suggested audience: High school students (grades 9–12), adaptable for introductory college courses
  • • Lesson Objective: Students will use evidence from the episode to explain how women in southeastern Nigeria exercised political authority and why they mobilized against British colonial rule in 1929.
